# Advice for New Developers in the Age of AI

This post is an expansion of a recent [Linkedin post](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7448346416449523712/?originTrackingId=ptTKCxgJegp4WsLjiLB24A%3D%3D) I made about some advice I gave to aspiring developers. 

After I made the post, I started thinking about these concepts further and decided to do a more in-depth essay on the topic, while rearranging some of my initial thoughts.

---

At a recent GDG event, I spoke with many aspiring software engineers and many of them were asking some version of the same question:

**How do I make it in the age of AI? **

My answer was something which they probably were not expecting:

**Focus on meta skills first, then core skills.**

A lot of advice for new developers today is tool-first: learn the latest model, the newest framework, the fastest workflow.

That matters. But it is not the foundation.

Tools change quickly. Specially in the AI age where new things come out almost everyday, making it impossible to keep up.

What compounds more slowly — and stays useful longer — is how well you **learn**, **read** and **think**. Those are meta skills. 

And in an AI-heavy world, they matter even more because they determine whether you use AI as leverage or as a crutch. **Marty Cagan, author of *Inspired*,** has made a similar point in the context of product and AI, *"The people who do well in major technology shifts are often the ones who can learn fast, think through hard problems, and keep empathy for customers."*

## Why meta skills matter more than most developers realize

Meta skills often look too basic to be important.

Reading. Thinking. Learning.

They sound almost obvious. But that is exactly why they get neglected. Because we take them for granted.

Most developers try to improve by collecting more information. Fewer try to improve **how** they absorb information, **how** they reason through it and **how** they update their mental models when they are wrong.

That difference becomes much more visible in the age of AI.

When code can be generated quickly, your edge is no longer just typing speed or API recall. Your edge becomes:

- how well you frame the problem
- how well you evaluate output
- how well you detect hidden assumptions
- how quickly you can learn a new concept deeply enough to use it responsibly

That is meta-skill territory.

### 1) Learn how to "Learn Better"

This is the first multiplier.

**Satya Nadella, the leader who reshaped Microsoft’s culture,** wrote in his first CEO message to Microsoft that *"He is defined by curiosity and a thirst for learning and that if you stop learning new things, you stop doing great and useful things."* That idea was not just personal philosophy — it became part of Microsoft’s broader growth-mindset culture.

The goal is not to know everything. The goal is to become someone who can **learn new things quickly & reliably**.

That means a few practical shifts:
- Stop confusing exposure with mastery
- Break topics into chunks you can revisit
- Test yourself instead of only rereading
- Revisit concepts across multiple contexts
- Build the habit of explaining what you learned in your own words

**Barbara Oakley, author of *Learning How to Learn*,** helped popularize many of these ideas for a broad audience. Her work emphasizes chunking, illusions of learning, memory techniques, and research-backed practices for mastering difficult subjects.

That matters because many beginners think they are learning when they are really just recognizing familiar words.

Instead of saying, "I watched three videos on coroutines," you start asking:
- Can I explain when to use them?
- Can I spot the tradeoff?
- Can I debug a broken implementation?
- Can I connect the concept to a real product need?

That is learning, not just content consumption.

### 2) Learn how to "Read More & Read Better"

Reading is underrated in software careers because the industry tends to celebrate building more than understanding.

I also made this mistake and never read any technical books for the first few years in my professional journey, which I still consider as one of the biggest regrets of my career.

But great developers are constantly reading:
documentation, codebases, architecture notes, bug reports, product feedback, RFCs, test failures, design discussions, and sometimes books well outside software.

**Charlie Munger, one of world's famous Investor,** was famous for treating reading as a core part of wise judgment.

Reading better means:
- Knowing when to skim and when to slow down
- Extracting structure, not just sentences
- Comparing multiple sources on the same topic
- Taking notes that capture decisions and tradeoffs
- Asking, "What changed in my understanding after reading this?"

This matters because AI can summarize text for you, but *it cannot build your judgment for you.*

A new developer who reads well learns faster from docs, reviews, incidents and other people’s mistakes. They also become easier to trust, because they stop missing important details hiding in plain sight.

### 3) Learn how to "Think Better"

Thinking better is partly about clarity and partly about honesty.

**Richard Feynman, Nobel Prize-winning physicist,** put it bluntly in his famous Caltech address: *"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."*

AI systems are useful, but they are also confident, fast and often plausible-looking. That combination can easily trick beginners into outsourcing judgment. If you do not have strong habits of thinking, you can mistake fluency for correctness.

Thinking better means:
- Separating evidence from confidence
- Checking assumptions before defending them
- Tracing cause and effect instead of guessing
- Asking what would disprove your current view
- Staying comfortable with "I don’t know, yet"

This is also where reflection matters.

Many early-career developers move too quickly from output to output. They write code, ship something, maybe fix a bug and move on. But the strongest growth often happens when you pause long enough to ask:
- Why did this design feel easy at first and painful later?
- What signal did I ignore?
- What would I do differently if I had to build this again?

AI can accelerate output. It does not automatically accelerate reflection.

## Why these meta skills are "AI-proof"

I do not love the phrase "AI-proof," because no skill is completely immune to change. Now that I think about it, I should not have used that term in my original post. 

The idea is not fight AI or building any resilience against it. Its more about growing to a point where it can function as an effective tool, while you maintain your *critical thinking, original ideas and curiosity*

Meta skills are durable because they help you adapt and they work across tools, domains and technology shifts.

That is also why this advice keeps showing up in very different places. Microsoft’s culture language emphasizes growth mindset. Marty Cagan emphasizes learning speed, empathy, and judgment in periods of technological change. Feynman emphasized intellectual honesty. These are different contexts, but the pattern is the same:

**The people who keep getting better are the ones who keep learning clearly.**

## Core skills still matter - a lot

Meta skills come first in this discussion because they determine how fast and how well you grow.

But they are not enough on their own.

You still need strong **core skills**.

### 1) Focus on architecture

Architecture is not just a senior-engineer topic.

It is how you learn to see structure, boundaries, responsibilities, and tradeoffs.

That means asking:
- Where should this logic live?
- What is this module responsible for?
- What coupling am I creating?
- What becomes harder to change if I do this?

AI can generate code. But you are the who will be owning the long-term consequences of structural decisions.

### 2) Learn to write clean, modular, testable code

This is one of the clearest ways to become more valuable early.

Clean, modular, testable code helps because it:
- Reduces cognitive load
- Makes debugging easier
- Makes collaboration easier
- Lets you change behavior without breaking everything
- Reveals design problems earlier

In practice, this means new developers should pay more attention to boundaries, naming, dependency management and testability than to cleverness.

Fast code generation does not always mean maintainable code.

### 3) Learn to see work at a product level

One of the fastest ways to stand out as a developer is to stop thinking only in tasks.

A product mindset asks:
- Who is this for?
- What user problem does it solve?
- What tradeoff are we making?
- How will we know this helped?

Here, **Marty Cagan, author of *Inspired*,** offers a useful lens: *technology exists to serve customer needs within business constraints, not just to implement requested features.*

That shift is huge for early-career developers.

When you start connecting engineering decisions to customer outcomes, your work gets better. You make better prioritization decisions. You ask better questions. You avoid spending days polishing something that does not matter.

And importantly, product thinking is hard to automate because it depends on context, empathy, tradeoffs and judgment.

## The real opportunity for new developers

AI is not removing the need for developers.

It is raising the premium on developers who can think clearly, learn continuously and build responsibly.

In short, start here:

**Meta skills**
- learn better
- read better
- think better

Then strengthen these:

**Core skills**
- architecture
- clean, modular, testable code
- product thinking

That combination is powerful because it compounds.

Meta skills help you improve faster.  
Core skills help you produce better work.  
Together, they make you much more resilient than chasing tools alone ever will.
